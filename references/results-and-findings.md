# Results and Findings

## When To Use

Use this reference for results sections, findings chapters, empirical reporting, table/figure commentary, and results-only revision.

## Core Function

The results or findings section presents selected evidence in a clear, organized, and credible way. It should guide the reader through what was found before broader interpretation is developed in the discussion.

## Results Versus Discussion

The boundary is field-sensitive. Some documents separate results and discussion; others combine them. Paper-based or multi-study documents may also present and interpret findings within each paper or study section. If the user asks for a results-only section, avoid extended interpretation, implications, or literature comparison unless the genre requires it.

Results usually answer:

- What was found?
- Where is the evidence?
- How is it organized?
- Which patterns are most relevant to the research questions?

Discussion usually answers:

- What do the findings mean?
- Why might they have occurred?
- How do they relate to prior work?
- What are the implications and limits?

## Recommended Structure

1. Brief advance organizer explaining the purpose, order, link to research questions, and type of evidence.
2. Organization by research question, objective, hypothesis, theme, case, variable, experiment, method, phase, analytic stage, or combined logic.
3. Presentation of key findings.
4. Evidence through tables, figures, quotes, examples, statistics, or excerpts.
5. Local summary after each major finding or subsection.
6. Transition to discussion or next result.

Use subheadings to improve flow and help readers see how findings are grouped.

## Three-Move Results Cycle

Many results sections repeat a small cycle:

1. Metatextual information: preview the point, link to the research question, provide needed background, refer back to the method, or point to a table or figure.
2. Presenting results: restate a hypothesis or research question where useful, explain what the data are, report findings, highlight data requiring attention, and present statistics, examples, visuals, quotations, or observed patterns.
3. Commenting on results: highlight a trend, comparison, notable value, limited interpretation, or transition.

If results and discussion are separated, keep Move 3 brief and save broader meaning, literature comparison, and implications for the discussion. If results and discussion are combined, Move 3 may include cautious comments on strengths, limitations, generalizability, or brief contribution claims.

## Selecting Results

Do not report everything simply because it was collected. Select results that:

- answer research questions;
- support the argument;
- show major patterns;
- clarify unexpected or negative findings;
- represent important variation;
- are needed for later discussion.

## Quantitative Reporting

Report quantitative findings with enough detail for interpretation:

- direction and size of effect or relationship;
- relevant descriptive statistics;
- inferential statistics when appropriate;
- confidence intervals or uncertainty where suitable;
- table or figure references;
- practical meaning without over-interpreting.

Useful language:

- Table 1 shows...
- The results indicate that...
- X was associated with...
- No statistically significant difference was observed...
- The largest change occurred in...
- These descriptive results show...
- approximately...
- just over...
- just below...
- nearly three-fifths...
- the proportion increased from X to Y...
- the value declined by...
- the difference between X and Y was...

## Qualitative Reporting

Qualitative findings usually need a balance of claim, evidence, and analytic commentary.

Possible pattern:

1. Name the theme or finding.
2. Explain what it means.
3. Provide a short excerpt or example.
4. Interpret the excerpt locally.
5. Link back to the research question or theme.

Useful language:

- A recurring theme was...
- Participants described...
- This pattern was particularly evident in...
- The extract illustrates...
- These responses suggest a tendency to...

## Tables And Figures

Use visuals to help the reader see patterns, not to decorate the section.

Check:

- every table or figure is introduced before or near first mention;
- titles are informative;
- labels and units are clear;
- the text highlights the main point rather than repeating every number;
- visuals are readable and consistently formatted;
- the same result is not redundantly reported in both prose and visual form unless needed.

Placement strategy:

- for simple data, present the visual first and describe the main pattern after it;
- for complex data, give a guiding sentence before the visual, then explain the main pattern after it;
- mention the table or figure before asking the reader to interpret it;
- avoid splitting small tables in a way that interrupts interpretation;
- use chapter-based numbering if that is the document convention;
- keep short tables intact where possible;
- avoid page breaks that separate a visual from its explanation;
- check that every in-text reference, number, label, caption, and unit matches the visual.

Useful visual-guiding language:

- Table X shows...
- Figure X illustrates...
- As shown in Table X...
- The results are summarized in...
- This comparison indicates...

Useful comparison language:

- compared with;
- in contrast;
- unlike;
- both groups;
- the difference between X and Y;
- the increase was greater than;
- the responses were similar.

## Tense Guidance

- Use past tense for results obtained in the study: "The analysis showed..."
- Use present tense for what a table or figure shows: "Table 2 shows..."
- Use present perfect when describing a pattern or development that continues to the present, if the genre supports it.

## Timing Guidance

- Draft results after analysis is stable enough to identify the key findings.
- Build and clean tables or figures before finalizing the prose.
- Draft the advance organizer after the internal order is clear.
- Write local summaries after result subsections are stable and before moving into broader discussion.

## Common Weak Patterns

- Presenting raw output without explaining what matters.
- Reporting every result in collection order instead of analytical order.
- Repeating the table in prose.
- Mixing major interpretation into a results-only section.
- Providing quotes without analytic commentary.
- Reporting themes as labels without evidence.
- Hiding negative, null, or unexpected results that matter for the argument.
- Using vague quantity words such as "many" or "some" without enough context.
- Referring to a table or figure too early or too late.
- Letting a visual carry the argument without prose explanation.

## Checklist

- Is the organization clear?
- Does each subsection answer a question or develop a finding?
- Are results selected rather than dumped?
- Are tables and figures integrated into the prose?
- Is the level of interpretation appropriate for the genre?
- Are quantitative results reported accurately and completely enough?
- Are qualitative examples interpreted rather than merely displayed?
- Are unexpected or negative findings handled transparently?
- Are visuals numbered, introduced, explained, and checked for consistency?
- Is present tense used for what a table or figure shows, where appropriate?
