# AI-Assisted Writing Integrity

## When To Use

Use this reference when the user asks how to use AI tools responsibly for academic writing, revision, language polishing, brainstorming, outlining, feedback, paraphrase checking, or documentation of AI assistance.

## Core Principle

AI tools may support the writing process, but the writer remains responsible for the argument, evidence, sources, interpretation, methods, findings, and final text. AI assistance should improve scholarly judgment, not replace it.

## Appropriate Uses

AI support is usually most useful for:

- brainstorming possible structures or research-question wordings;
- reverse-outlining a draft;
- identifying unclear logic, weak cohesion, or missing transitions;
- suggesting alternative wording for the writer's own draft;
- checking grammar, concision, or academic register;
- generating checklists for revision;
- helping compare possible section structures.

The writer should verify every suggestion against the evidence, target genre, field conventions, and source material.

## Use By Writing Stage

AI assistance can be useful at different stages when the writer keeps control:

- ideation: generate possible angles, dimensions, or narrower areas;
- research-question refinement: compare question forms against feasibility and field knowledge;
- literature preparation: generate search terms, synthesis questions, or matrix headings, then verify sources independently;
- outlining: test possible section structures against the actual evidence;
- draft feedback: identify unclear logic, missing transitions, redundancy, or weak paragraph order;
- language support: suggest wording, collocations, grammar explanations, or concise alternatives for the writer's own text;
- revision: reverse-outline the section and check whether claims, evidence, and transitions align;
- proofreading: identify local errors without silently replacing the writer's draft.

## High-Risk Uses

Avoid or treat with extreme caution:

- submitting AI-generated prose as the writer's own work;
- asking AI to invent citations, studies, methods, data, findings, or quotations;
- trusting AI summaries without reading the original source;
- using AI paraphrase to disguise close copying;
- using translation or paraphrase tools in ways that distort technical meaning or authorial voice;
- pasting confidential, sensitive, unpublished, or restricted material into tools without permission;
- using AI-generated figures, images, data analysis, or interpretation when the target venue restricts such use.

## Prompting For Revision

Good prompts define:

- discipline or field;
- section type;
- audience;
- purpose;
- desired mode: diagnose, outline, revise, or polish;
- scope or word limit;
- target tone or register;
- constraints, such as preserve citations or do not add new claims;
- whether citations should be avoided, preserved, or checked separately;
- whether the tool should explain changes.

Useful prompt pattern:

> Diagnose the paragraph below for academic cohesion and register. Do not rewrite it yet. Identify the three highest-impact issues, explain why they matter, and suggest one revision strategy for each.

For revision:

> Revise the paragraph below for academic register and sentence flow. Preserve all citations, technical terms, claim strength, and meaning. Do not add new evidence or claims. After the revision, briefly list the main changes.

To keep the output auditable, ask the tool to:

- list issues before rewriting;
- explain each suggested change;
- point to the sentence or phrase being discussed;
- provide examples rather than replacing a whole section;
- preserve technical meaning and authorial voice;
- separate grammar, cohesion, concision, and claim-strength feedback.

## Documentation And Transparency

When AI assistance matters to the writing process, keep a simple AI-use log:

- tool name and version if known;
- date of use;
- purpose of use;
- prompt or task description;
- relevant output or summary of output;
- what was accepted, rejected, or changed;
- why suggestions were accepted or rejected;
- how the writer verified the result;
- brief reflection on how prompting became more specific, constrained, or useful over time;
- whether disclosure or acknowledgement is required by the target context.

If disclosure is required, describe the role of AI accurately. Do not imply that AI produced research, data, analysis, or final claims if it was used only for language feedback.

Update the log soon after meaningful AI use. Retrospective reconstruction is less reliable and can blur the difference between AI output, writer decisions, and final text.

## Paraphrase And Source Use

AI-assisted paraphrase does not remove the need to cite the original source. The writer must still understand the source, represent it accurately, cite it, and integrate it into the argument.

Check AI-assisted paraphrases for:

- accurate meaning;
- sufficient transformation of wording and structure;
- preserved technical terms where needed;
- clear citation;
- no invented detail;
- no stronger claim than the source supports.

## Translation And Language Polishing

Use translation and polishing tools cautiously:

- start from the writer's own meaning;
- preserve technical terms;
- check whether the output changes claim strength;
- avoid accepting fluent but inaccurate phrasing;
- revise the result so it matches the writer's evidence and disciplinary voice.

Phrase-level or sentence-level support is safer than whole-paragraph or whole-section replacement when authorship, assessment, or originality matters, unless the target policy clearly allows broader language support.

## Policy Awareness

AI-use rules can differ across venues, journals, conferences, programs, and publishers. Before submission, verify the current rule for:

- whether AI assistance must be disclosed;
- whether AI tools can be credited or cited;
- whether AI-generated images or figures are restricted;
- whether AI may be used for translation or language editing;
- whether confidential material may be entered into external tools.

Do not state a policy as fact unless the user provides the current rule or asks for a separate policy lookup.

## Checklist

- Did the writer keep control of the argument and evidence?
- Were all citations, summaries, and claims verified?
- Was AI used as feedback rather than as a substitute author?
- Were accepted suggestions revised to fit the writer's meaning and field?
- Were rejected suggestions ignored for a clear reason?
- Was any AI-assisted paraphrase still properly cited?
- Was confidential or restricted material protected?
- Was AI use documented if required?
- Were target-context disclosure rules checked before submission?
