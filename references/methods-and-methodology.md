# Methods and Methodology

## When To Use

Use this reference for methods sections, methodology chapters, research design descriptions, and proposal method plans.

## Core Function

The methods or methodology section should show how the study was designed and conducted, why those choices were appropriate, and why the resulting evidence can be trusted within the limits of the design.

## Methods Versus Methodology

- Methods are the specific procedures, instruments, datasets, sampling choices, and analytical steps.
- Methodology is the rationale that connects research questions, design, assumptions, procedures, and trustworthiness.

Short articles may emphasize methods. Longer theses and proposals usually need more methodological justification.

## Recommended Structure

Possible sections include:

0. Advance organizer explaining the design logic and section order.
1. Research questions or objectives, if they need to be restated.
2. Research paradigm, framework, stance, design, or approach.
3. Research context or setting.
4. Participants, materials, corpus, dataset, or cases.
5. Sampling or selection rationale.
6. Instruments, measures, protocols, or data sources.
7. Data collection procedures.
8. Data management, processing, or preparation.
9. Data analysis procedures.
10. Reliability, validity, credibility, dependability, or trustworthiness.
11. Ethical considerations.
12. Limitations of the design.

Not every study needs every heading. Choose headings that match the design.

For complex designs, add a design map that links each research question to:

- data or material;
- method of collection or selection;
- analytical procedure;
- expected output;
- section where the answer will appear.

Possible organization patterns include:

- chronological order, when procedure sequence matters;
- research-question order, when each question uses different evidence or analysis;
- method order, when several methods are used;
- stage or phase order, when the design develops across phases;
- process order, when the reader must understand a workflow.

Use clear headings so readers can locate procedures, rationale, and evidence quickly. Weak or vague headings make the design harder to judge and can obscure how research questions connect to methods.

## Design Justification

A method description answers "what was done." A method justification answers "why this was the right way to answer the question."

Useful language:

- This design was selected because...
- A qualitative approach was appropriate because...
- The dataset was chosen to capture...
- The sampling strategy allowed the study to...
- This procedure reduced the risk of...
- The analysis focused on... because...
- Alternative approaches were not used because...
- This method was preferred over... because...
- Following the conceptual framework, this section explains...
- To ensure reliability, validity, or trustworthiness...

When relevant, briefly explain why plausible alternatives were not chosen. This helps the reader see that the method is a reasoned design choice, not merely a convenient procedure.

Justifications may draw on:

- research aims or questions;
- literature and conceptual framework;
- data needs;
- disciplinary conventions;
- practical constraints;
- ethical constraints;
- target-context expectations.

## Alignment

Check alignment among:

- research questions;
- design;
- data or material;
- sampling or selection;
- analysis;
- claims made in results and discussion.

A common weakness is using methods that can describe a pattern but not support causal or general claims.

## Quantitative Methods

Common expected content:

- design type;
- variables or constructs;
- sampling and sample characteristics;
- instruments and measurement;
- procedure;
- statistical analysis;
- assumptions and diagnostics where relevant;
- reliability and validity evidence;
- ethical handling of participants or data.

Avoid reporting only software names. Explain analytical choices.

## Qualitative Methods

Common expected content:

- research paradigm or approach when relevant;
- site, case, corpus, or participant selection;
- data collection method;
- interview, observation, document, or coding procedure;
- analytical framework;
- coding or interpretation process;
- reflexivity, credibility, and trustworthiness;
- ethical safeguards.

Avoid vague statements such as "themes were found" without explaining how analysis was conducted.

## Mixed Methods

Explain:

- why mixing methods is necessary;
- which design is used;
- which component has priority;
- whether integration occurs during design, analysis, or interpretation;
- how the strands answer the research questions together.

## Reliability, Validity, And Trustworthiness

Use terms appropriate to the design.

Possible strategies:

- piloting;
- triangulation;
- transparent coding;
- intercoder discussion or agreement where suitable;
- member checking or transcript verification where suitable;
- audit trail;
- sample-size or saturation rationale;
- instrument validation;
- bias management;
- sensitivity analysis;
- clear inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Do not merely name validity or reliability. Explain how it was supported.

## Ethics And Data Protection

Ethics sections should be as specific as the study requires. They may address:

- ethical approval or review where applicable;
- informed consent;
- confidentiality and anonymity;
- data protection and storage;
- handling of sensitive, restricted, or identifying material;
- participant risk, withdrawal, and debriefing where relevant.

The section should show how ethical risks were anticipated and managed, not simply state that the study was ethical.

## Tense And Voice

- Use past tense for completed procedures: "Data were collected..."
- Use present tense for stable facts, chapter organization, or general principles: "The section explains..."
- Passive voice can emphasize procedures; active voice can clarify researcher decisions.
- Avoid hiding responsibility when the researcher's decision matters.
- Use present or past tense consistently when describing instruments, datasets, or frameworks.
- Use future tense only for previewing later sections or planned work in a proposal.

End the methods or methodology section by summarizing the overall methodological picture rather than repeating every detail. The closing should show how the design can answer the research questions and prepare the reader for the results or analysis section.

## Timing Guidance

- Draft core procedures during or soon after the research process, while details are still accurate.
- Finalize the section after data collection, selection, and analysis decisions are stable.
- Revisit the section after drafting results to confirm that methods and reported findings align.

## Common Weak Patterns

- Listing procedures with no rationale.
- Describing instruments but not explaining what they measure.
- Omitting sampling or selection criteria.
- Claiming representativeness without support.
- Confusing methodology with literature review.
- Providing no advance organizer for a complex design.
- Reporting analysis too vaguely.
- Adding trustworthiness terms without showing evidence.
- Ignoring ethical handling of participants, data, or sensitive material.
- Presenting limitations defensively instead of showing how risks were managed.

## Checklist

- Are research questions and methods aligned?
- Is the design named and justified?
- Are participants, data, cases, or materials described clearly?
- Are selection criteria transparent?
- Are procedures reproducible enough for the document type?
- Is analysis explained step by step?
- Are reliability, validity, or trustworthiness addressed?
- Are ethical issues handled appropriately?
- Are limitations of the design acknowledged without undermining the whole study?
