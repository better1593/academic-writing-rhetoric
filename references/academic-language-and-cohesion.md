# Academic Language and Cohesion

## When To Use

Use this reference for language polishing, paragraph flow, cohesion diagnosis, reporting verbs, citation grammar, stance, hedging, paraphrase, and academic register.

## Core Principle

Cohesion is not the same as adding more connectors. Strong academic flow comes from controlled information order, lexical chains, clear reference, purposeful repetition, accurate connectors, and consistent stance.

Coherence concerns the larger logic of the argument: whether the section has a purpose, the ideas are ordered meaningfully, and the reader can see the direction of the discussion. Cohesion concerns the sentence-level and paragraph-level links that help the reader move through that logic. A text can be grammatical but incoherent, and it can contain many connectors while still lacking cohesion.

## Information Flow

Academic paragraphs usually move from familiar information to new information:

- Start with a topic sentence that links back to the section aim or previous paragraph.
- Place old or expected information early in the sentence.
- Place new, complex, or important information later in the sentence.
- Use the end of one sentence to prepare the beginning of the next.
- Use summary nouns such as "this pattern", "this limitation", "this distinction", or "this assumption" to connect ideas without repeating whole clauses.

Practical subject diagnostic:

- underline the grammatical subject of each sentence;
- ask whether the subject links back to the previous sentence or paragraph focus;
- check whether new information at the end of one sentence becomes familiar information in the next;
- revise sudden subject shifts by adding a bridge, changing sentence order, or repeating the key term.

Weak pattern:

- Each sentence introduces a new topic without linking it to the previous one.

Better pattern:

- Sentence 1 states the claim.
- Sentence 2 explains the mechanism or reason.
- Sentence 3 gives evidence.
- Sentence 4 interprets the evidence or links it to the next point.

## Connectors

Use connectors only when they state the real logical relationship.

### Addition

- in addition
- moreover
- furthermore
- another important factor is

Avoid using additive connectors to join unrelated points. A list of "moreover" sentences is not synthesis.

### Contrast

- however
- nevertheless
- by contrast
- in contrast
- whereas
- while

Use "on the other hand" for a parallel alternative. Use "on the contrary" only to reject or correct a previous idea.

### Cause and Result

- because
- since
- therefore
- thus
- consequently
- as a result
- this suggests that
- this may explain why

"Because" is not inherently informal. The issue is whether the causal relationship is precise and whether the sentence is appropriately structured.

### Example or Specification

- for example
- for instance
- specifically
- in particular
- this can be seen in

Do not let examples replace analysis. After an example, explain what it demonstrates.

### Comparison

- similarly
- likewise
- compared with
- in the same way
- by comparison

### Summary

- overall
- in summary
- taken together
- collectively

### Emphasis

- in particular
- more specifically
- especially
- notably

### Concession

- although
- even though
- despite
- nevertheless

Use concessive connectors when both ideas remain valid but one complicates the other.

## Connector Misuse

- Do not begin every paragraph with a generic connector.
- Do not use a connector when the relationship is already obvious.
- Do not use "besides" in formal academic prose when "in addition", "moreover", or "furthermore" is intended.
- Do not use "however" to connect ideas that are not actually in contrast.
- Do not use "therefore" when the evidence only suggests a possibility.

## Lexical Cohesion

Good academic writing controls repetition:

- Repeat key technical terms when precision matters.
- Use synonyms only when they preserve the same meaning.
- Use superordinate terms to move from specific examples to categories.
- Use summary nouns to condense previous claims.
- Avoid repeating vague words such as "things", "aspects", "issues", or "important" without specification.

Weak pattern:

- Repeating the same general word because no conceptual relationship has been built.

Better pattern:

- Repeat the key term, then develop it through category, contrast, cause, or implication.

## Reference Words And Summary Nouns

Avoid bare "this", "these", or "that" when the reference could be unclear. Use "this + noun" to name how the reader should understand the previous idea.

Useful forms:

- this finding;
- this limitation;
- this pattern;
- this assumption;
- this process;
- this contrast;
- this explanation.

Summary nouns can be neutral or evaluative. For example, "this result" simply refers back, while "this limitation" tells the reader that the previous point should be treated as a constraint.

Check pronouns carefully. If "it", "they", "this", or "these" could refer to more than one idea, replace the pronoun with a specific noun phrase.

## Formal Academic Register

Prefer precise verbs over informal or vague phrasing:

- "investigate" or "examine" instead of "look at"
- "address" instead of "deal with"
- "develop" or "propose" instead of "come up with"
- "identify" instead of "find out"
- "increase" or "decrease" instead of "go up" or "go down"
- "demonstrate" or "show" instead of "prove" when certainty is limited

Avoid conversational fillers:

- "a lot of"
- "kind of"
- "sort of"
- "things"
- "very important"
- "nowadays" when unsupported

## Active And Passive Voice

Passive voice is common in methods, results, and literature-based writing because it can foreground procedures, data, or findings rather than the researcher.

Useful passive patterns:

- Data were collected from...
- The samples were analysed using...
- The responses were coded according to...
- The results were compared with...

Passive voice should not be used everywhere. Overuse can make prose indirect, flat, or hard to follow. Active voice is often clearer, and active voice does not always require "I" or "we".

Useful non-first-person active patterns:

- This section examines...
- The analysis compares...
- The findings suggest...
- The study identifies...

Choose voice according to focus:

- use passive when procedure or result matters more than actor;
- use active when the agent, decision, or argument needs to be clear;
- avoid using passive voice to hide responsibility for interpretive choices.

## Stance and Hedging

Academic claims usually need calibrated certainty.

Stronger:

- The findings demonstrate that...
- The results indicate that...
- This evidence supports the claim that...

Moderate:

- The findings suggest that...
- This pattern may indicate...
- One possible explanation is...
- This result is consistent with...

Careful limitation:

- This interpretation should be treated with caution because...
- The evidence does not establish...
- Further work is needed to determine...

Do not hedge so much that the claim disappears. Do not overclaim beyond the evidence.

## Literature Review Flow

Literature review paragraphs need both cohesion and synthesis. A useful sequence is:

1. State the issue, pattern, or claim about the literature.
2. Bring in evidence from one or more sources.
3. Compare or contrast the sources.
4. Evaluate what the pattern shows and what it does not show.
5. Link the paragraph back to the research gap, question, or next issue.

This order keeps the writer's argument in control. Avoid letting citations become the paragraph structure.

## Agreement, Disagreement, And Evaluation

Use disagreement carefully. A critique should identify the exact limitation and support it with evidence, not simply dismiss prior work.

Useful stance patterns:

- X provides useful evidence for..., but it does not explain...
- While this approach clarifies..., it gives less attention to...
- These studies are consistent in showing..., although they differ in...
- This claim should be interpreted cautiously because...

When criticizing, combine precise reporting verbs, evidence, and hedging where appropriate.

## Reporting Verbs

Reporting verbs shape the writer's stance toward cited work.

Neutral:

- state
- describe
- report
- observe
- note

Knowledge-building:

- demonstrate
- show
- establish
- identify
- reveal

Interpretive:

- argue
- suggest
- propose
- contend
- maintain

Critical or contrastive:

- challenge
- question
- overlook
- underestimate
- fail to account for

Choose the verb according to what the cited author does and how you evaluate it.

## Citation Tense

- Use present tense for generally accepted knowledge, ongoing relevance, or author argument: "Smith argues..."
- Use past tense for completed methods, specific results, or historical studies: "Smith found..."
- Use present perfect to summarize an area of continuing work: "Researchers have examined..."

Keep tense choices consistent inside a paragraph unless the meaning changes.

## Paraphrase and Quotation

Good paraphrase changes more than isolated words:

- understand the original idea;
- close the original text;
- restate the idea using a new sentence structure;
- change word class where possible;
- preserve technical terms that cannot be replaced accurately;
- cite the idea;
- integrate it into your own argument.

Use direct quotation only when exact wording matters, such as definitions, contested terminology, or memorable phrasing. In most research writing, paraphrase and synthesis are more useful than quotation.

Do not drop quotations into a paragraph without framing. Introduce the reason for the quotation, quote only what is needed, and explain how the quoted wording supports, complicates, or defines the point being made.

## Section Transitions

Transitions between sections should show conceptual movement, not merely add a connector.

Useful patterns:

- Having reviewed X, the next section examines Y.
- This section has shown that...
- The following section turns from X to Y.
- These findings raise the question of...
- The discussion now moves from description to interpretation.

Before proofreading, revise for coherence and cohesion: section purpose, paragraph order, old-to-new flow, key-term consistency, and citation integration. Grammar polishing works best after the argument path is already clear.

## Paragraph Diagnosis

Check each paragraph:

- What is the controlling idea?
- Does the first sentence prepare the reader?
- Does each sentence connect to the previous one?
- Are citations integrated into the writer's argument?
- Is there analysis after evidence?
- Is the final sentence doing useful work?
- Are connectors accurate and not excessive?
- Are key terms repeated deliberately?

## Common Weak Patterns

- Connector stacking: too many transitions without real logical development.
- Citation dumping: several studies listed with no synthesis.
- Synonym drift: changing terms until the concept becomes unclear.
- Overgeneralization: claiming too much from limited evidence.
- Informal phrasal verbs where precise academic verbs are expected.
- Empty evaluation: "This study is important" without explaining why.
- Mechanical paragraph endings: every paragraph ends with "more research is needed."

## Quick Polish Checklist

- Replace vague nouns with precise concepts.
- Replace informal verbs with formal equivalents.
- Remove unnecessary connectors.
- Add connectors only where the relation needs help.
- Use hedging where evidence is limited.
- Strengthen claims where evidence is strong.
- Ensure each citation is interpreted.
- Check that paragraph endings build the next step.
