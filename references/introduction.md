# Introduction

## When To Use

Use this reference for article introductions, thesis introductions, proposal openings, and confirmation-report openings. The goal is to move the reader from a broad research area to a specific problem, gap, purpose, and document map.

## Core Function

An introduction should answer four questions:

1. What is the larger area or problem?
2. What is known, unresolved, limited, or contested?
3. What will this study or paper do?
4. Why does that work matter?

## Recommended Structure

### Move 1: Establish The Territory

- introduce the research area;
- define key terms if needed;
- show importance, relevance, scale, or urgency;
- bring in selected key prior studies when they help bridge background and gap;
- identify the audience or field affected by the problem.

Useful language:

- Recent work on X has focused on...
- X has become an important issue in...
- In the context of Y, X refers to...
- Understanding X is important because...

### Move 2: Establish The Niche

- identify a gap, limitation, contradiction, unresolved question, neglected context, methodological weakness, or practical problem;
- show why the gap matters;
- avoid claiming that "no research exists" unless this has been carefully verified.

Gap types:

- limited context;
- limited population or sample;
- limited method;
- inconsistent findings;
- underdeveloped theory;
- missing mechanism;
- lack of integration across areas;
- practical problem not explained by current research.

Useful language:

- However, little is known about...
- Previous studies have tended to focus on..., rather than...
- Existing research has not fully explained...
- These studies provide valuable insights, but they do not address...
- The relationship between X and Y remains unclear.
- This leaves open the question of...

Useful gap-language categories:

- inadequacy verbs: overlook, neglect, underestimate, fail to consider;
- limited-work phrases: little research, few studies, limited evidence, no systematic account;
- evaluative adjectives: incomplete, inconclusive, contested, insufficient, unconvincing;
- uncertainty forms: it remains unclear, it is not yet known, the evidence is mixed;
- method-limit forms: existing approaches rely on..., earlier methods do not account for...

### Move 3: Occupy The Niche

- state the aim, research questions, hypothesis, argument, or contribution;
- introduce the approach or data if relevant;
- preview the structure of the document.

Useful language:

- This study investigates...
- This paper argues that...
- To address this gap, the study examines...
- The analysis draws on...
- The article is organized as follows...

Language choices often shift across the moves:

- Move 1 often uses present or present perfect tense to show current relevance or accumulated work.
- Move 2 often uses evaluative, limiting, or uncertainty language to establish the gap.
- Move 3 often uses present tense to state what the current paper or study does.

## Expected Content

An effective introduction usually includes:

- research problem, need, or opportunity;
- enough background and selected literature to justify the study;
- an evidence-based gap, limitation, contradiction, or unresolved issue;
- aim, objectives, research questions, or hypotheses;
- brief methodology or approach preview where useful;
- expected significance or contribution;
- roadmap for longer documents.

Contribution may be framed as contribution to knowledge, method, practice, policy, professional understanding, or interpretation. The claim should be specific enough to be tested later in the discussion and conclusion.

## Aims, Objectives, And Research Questions

- An aim states the overall purpose.
- Objectives break the aim into achievable steps.
- Research questions state what the study seeks to answer.
- Hypotheses state expected relationships or outcomes, usually in more deductive designs.

Keep these aligned. A common weakness is stating broad aims but using narrow methods that cannot answer them.

Objectives are often numbered in longer documents. They should correspond to research stages, major tasks, chapters, or analytical steps rather than becoming a disconnected list.

## Definition Strategy

Define terms when they are contested, technical, used differently across fields, or central to the argument.

A useful definition pattern:

- term;
- general class;
- distinguishing detail;
- relevance to the study.

Example pattern:

- In this study, X refers to a form of Y characterized by Z.

Definitions should reflect disciplinary or specialist use, not only dictionary meaning. A useful definition shows why the term matters for the study's argument, scope, or method.

## Structure Map

A map is useful in longer documents, but it should not become a list of obvious section titles. It should show how the sections develop the argument.

Useful language:

- The next section reviews...
- The methodology section explains...
- The results section presents...
- The discussion then interprets...
- The conclusion considers...

For paper-based or multi-study documents, the introduction has extra work:

- identify the shared thread across papers or studies;
- explain how each paper or study addresses part of the research problem;
- justify the sequence of papers or studies;
- preview how the final synthesis will integrate them.

For each paper or study, map the gap, question, method, main claim, contribution, and relation to the whole project.

## Writing Strategies

- Start broad, but do not stay broad for too long.
- Move from importance to gap to purpose.
- Use literature selectively; the introduction is not a full literature review.
- Make the gap specific enough that the reader understands the need for the study.
- Avoid exaggerated novelty claims.
- State the contribution in a way that can be supported later.
- In long documents, preview the whole structure; in short papers, keep the map concise.
- Revisit the introduction late in the project so the aims, methods, findings, discussion, and contribution match the final document.

## Common Weak Patterns

- Opening with generic claims that could apply to almost any topic.
- Providing background without identifying a problem.
- Claiming a gap but not showing why it matters.
- Presenting several gaps without choosing the one this study addresses.
- Listing aims, objectives, and questions that do not match each other.
- Promising a contribution that the methods and data cannot deliver.
- Using vague value claims such as "important", "significant", or "valuable" without explanation.
- In paper-based or multi-study documents, failing to explain how the parts connect.

## Checklist

- Is the research territory clear?
- Is the problem or gap specific?
- Does the gap follow from prior work or practical need?
- Is the aim stated directly?
- Are objectives and research questions aligned?
- Is the necessity of the study clear?
- Is the approach feasible and grounded in theory, evidence, or method?
- Is the study positioned as needed, different, or better in a supportable way?
- Are key terms defined only where necessary?
- Is the contribution plausible and not overstated?
- Does the structure map help the reader understand the argument?
- For paper-based or multi-study documents, are the shared thread and sequence clear?
- Does the introduction prepare later sections rather than merely announce them?
