# Abstract

## When To Use

Use this reference for abstracts, extended abstracts, thesis summaries, article abstracts, conference abstracts, titles, and keywords.

## Core Function

An abstract is a self-contained compression of the whole study or argument. It should help readers quickly understand the problem, purpose, approach, main results or claims, and significance.

Check the target word limit before drafting. If no specific limit is available, write a concise abstract that includes the necessary moves without turning into a section-by-section summary.

For long documents, front matter also helps readers navigate the work. Its exact order, required declarations, word limits, and formatting should follow the current institution, department, publisher, or venue requirements.

Common front-matter checks include:

- title page;
- abstract;
- acknowledgements where appropriate;
- table of contents;
- lists of figures, tables, abbreviations, or symbols where needed;
- references and appendices in the required order.

## Common Abstract Moves

1. Background or context.
2. Problem, gap, or need.
3. Aim, research question, or argument.
4. Method, data, material, or approach.
5. Main results, findings, or claims.
6. Contribution, implication, or significance.

Not every abstract uses every move with equal weight. Empirical abstracts usually need method and findings. Conceptual abstracts may emphasize argument and contribution.

## Article Abstract

Article abstracts are usually compact and direct:

- state the problem quickly;
- identify the aim or claim;
- summarize the method or analytical approach;
- report the main findings;
- state the contribution or implication.

Avoid excessive background.

## Thesis Or Long-Document Abstract

A longer abstract may need to represent the entire document:

- research context;
- problem or gap;
- aims or questions;
- design and data;
- main findings across the document;
- overall contribution and implications.

It should still read as one coherent summary, not a chapter list.

Long-document abstracts often make research questions, research design, transitions, evidential language, cautious phrasing, and contextual setup more explicit than article abstracts. They may need more space for how the study was conducted and how major findings connect across sections.

## Drafting Strategy

- Write the abstract late, after the argument, methods, findings, discussion, and conclusion are stable.
- Use one compact sentence or short paragraph for each major move.
- Prioritize major findings and contribution over procedural detail.
- Avoid simply describing each section.
- Check same-field abstracts to learn typical move order, tense choices, and level of detail.
- Revise the abstract after any major change to aims, methods, results, or contribution.

## Title

A strong academic title is specific, searchable, and aligned with the document's contribution.

Possible title strategies:

- topic plus method or context;
- topic plus key relationship;
- concept plus case;
- finding-oriented title for articles where allowed.

Avoid:

- overly broad titles;
- unexplained acronyms;
- clever phrasing that hides the topic;
- titles that promise more than the study delivers.

## Keywords

Keywords should help readers and databases locate the work.

Choose terms that represent:

- main topic;
- key concept or theory;
- method or approach;
- context, population, material, or setting;
- field-specific terminology.

Avoid repeating only words already obvious from the title unless they are essential search terms.

## Language Strategies

- Use present tense for the problem, purpose, or contribution.
- Use past tense for completed methods and findings.
- Use precise nouns and verbs.
- Keep the abstract self-contained.
- Minimize citations unless the genre requires them.
- Define or avoid abbreviations that readers may not know.
- Do not include details that do not appear in the main document.

Useful language:

- This study examines...
- The paper investigates...
- Drawing on..., the analysis shows...
- The findings indicate...
- The study contributes to...
- These results suggest...

## Common Weak Patterns

- Too much background before the aim.
- No clear method or evidence base.
- Vague findings such as "important implications are discussed."
- Claims that are stronger than the main document supports.
- Abstract structured as a table of contents rather than an argument summary.
- Unnecessary chapter references or mechanical chapter-by-chapter summary.
- Undefined abbreviations.
- Citations, footnotes, or detailed examples that distract from the summary.

## Checklist

- Is the abstract self-contained?
- Is the research problem or need clear?
- Is the aim or argument explicit?
- Is the method or evidence base identified?
- Are the main findings or claims stated, not merely promised?
- Is the contribution or implication clear?
- Are title and keywords aligned with the document?
- Does the abstract accurately represent the final text?
- Does it meet the target length and formatting requirements?
- Have front-matter order, declarations, and formatting requirements been checked against the current target rules?
